# Chapter 18 — Tailoring

**After this chapter you should be able to:**
- explain what tailoring is, why it matters and who does it;
- describe what can be tailored and the steps of the tailoring process;
- identify situational factors that drive tailoring decisions;
- recognise good and bad tailoring in scenarios.

## 18.1 What tailoring is

**Tailoring** is the deliberate adaptation of the approach, governance, processes, methods and artifacts to fit the project's context — its objectives, complexity, risks, stakeholders, team, organisation and environment. The PMBOK® Guide is a body of knowledge, not a methodology; **every project needs tailoring**.

**Why tailor?**
- Deliver **more value** by spending effort where it matters (and not on unnecessary documentation or meetings).
- **Increase commitment** from the team, who help shape the way of working.
- Improve **efficiency** and **responsiveness** to change.
- Meet **compliance** and **governance** needs proportionately (more rigour where risk is higher).
- Make the approach **customer- and context-oriented**.

**Who tailors?** The project manager collaborates with the **team**, **sponsor**, **PMO** and other governance bodies. Tailoring decisions are documented (often in the project management plan or the team's working agreement) and **revisited** as the project evolves. Tailoring must stay within **organisational policies and mandatory requirements** — you can't tailor away a legal obligation.

## 18.2 What can be tailored

| Element | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Life cycle and development approach** | Predictive, adaptive or hybrid; number and type of phases; iteration length; release cadence |
| **Processes** | Add (e.g., extra safety reviews), remove (e.g., formal procurement when nothing is bought), modify (e.g., lighter change control for low-impact changes), blend (combine processes), align (with organisational standards) |
| **Engagement** | Who is involved and how (people, empowerment, integration of contractors and partners) |
| **Tools** | Scheduling software, collaboration tools, AI tools — chosen by the team within organisational constraints |
| **Methods and artifacts** | Which techniques and documents are used, and how formal they are |
| **Governance** | Gate reviews, reporting frequency, approval thresholds |
| **Performance domains** | Depth of attention to each domain based on the project's needs |

## 18.3 The tailoring process

A common four-step sequence:

1. **Select the initial development approach** — using the factors in Chapter 5 (product, project, organisation). Assess suitability (e.g., agile suitability filter).
2. **Tailor for the organisation** — align with the organisation's policies, PMO requirements, governance, approved tools and processes; the PMO or value delivery office may review and approve tailoring.
3. **Tailor for the project** — consider the **product/deliverable** (compliance, criticality, type, industry, uncertainty, complexity), the **project team** (size, location, experience, culture, access to the customer) and the **culture** (buy-in, trust, empowerment, organisational culture).
4. **Implement ongoing improvement** — review tailoring at phase gates, retrospectives and lessons learned; adjust as the project and team learn.

## 18.4 Situational factors that drive tailoring

| Factor | Pushes toward more formality / predictive elements | Pushes toward lighter / adaptive elements |
|---|---|---|
| Regulatory and compliance requirements | High (medical devices, aviation, nuclear) | Low |
| Criticality / safety | Life-critical | Low impact |
| Requirements stability | Stable, well understood | Uncertain, emerging |
| Cost of change | High (physical construction) | Low (software) |
| Team size and distribution | Large, distributed, many vendors | Small, co-located |
| Team experience | Inexperienced with adaptive practices | Experienced, self-organising |
| Stakeholder availability | Limited, infrequent | Frequent, engaged product owner |
| Contract type | Fixed-price with defined scope | Collaborative, incremental contracts |
| Organisational culture | Hierarchical, low trust | Empowered, high trust |
| Project size and duration | Large, long | Small, short |

## 18.5 Tailoring the performance domains — examples

| Domain | Tailoring questions |
|---|---|
| Governance | How many gates? Who approves what? How formal is change control? What reporting does the steering committee need? |
| Scope | WBS, backlog or both? How detailed are requirements up front? How will acceptance work? |
| Schedule | Detailed CPM schedule, release plan, Kanban flow? Iteration length? |
| Finance | Fixed budget vs incremental funding? EVM required? Level of estimate accuracy? |
| Stakeholders | How many and how diverse? Formal communications plan or information radiators? |
| Resources | Internal team, vendors or blended? Co-located or virtual? Procurement rules? |
| Risk | Qualitative only, or quantitative analysis too? Risk-adjusted backlog? Size of reserves? |

## 18.6 Good vs bad tailoring

**Good tailoring** is intentional, justified by the context, agreed with the right people, documented, compliant, and revisited.

**Bad tailoring** includes:
- Skipping essential controls (e.g., no change control on a fixed-price contract; no testing for a safety-critical product).
- Copying another project's approach without considering differences.
- "Agile" as an excuse for no planning, no documentation and no accountability.
- Heavy documentation and approvals on a small, low-risk, fast-moving initiative.
- Tailoring that breaches organisational policy or the law.

> **Exam tip.** When a question asks what to do at the start of a project in an unfamiliar context (new client, new industry, new team), the best answer often involves **assessing the context and tailoring** the approach with the team and stakeholders — not applying a standard methodology blindly, and not abandoning all structure.

What is the primary purpose of tailoring?
A) To adapt the approach, governance and processes to the project's context
B) To remove all documentation from the project
C) To apply every PMBOK Guide process to every project
D) To allow the project manager to ignore any organisational policies that slow the project down
= A
? Tailoring adapts the way of working to the context. It neither applies everything blindly nor removes necessary controls or policies.

Which is the first step of the tailoring process?
A) Select the initial development approach
B) Implement ongoing improvement of the approach
C) Tailor for the project team's culture
D) Archive the lessons learned
= A
? The usual sequence is: select the initial development approach, tailor for the organisation, tailor for the project, then implement ongoing improvement.

A small internal project with low risk and no external suppliers is required by the PMO to complete a 40-page procurement plan template. What is the best course of action?

C) Ignore the PMO's requirement without telling anyone, since the project is small and low-risk

= A
? Tailoring for the organisation means working with the PMO to adapt requirements to the context, documenting the rationale, rather than ignoring policy or producing waste.
