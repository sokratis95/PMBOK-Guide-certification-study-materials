# Chapter 7 — Governance Performance Domain

**After this chapter you should be able to:**
- explain what project governance is and what good governance looks like;
- describe the purpose, key inputs and outputs of the nine Governance processes;
- choose an appropriate contract type and sourcing approach;
- apply integrated change control in predictive, adaptive and hybrid contexts;
- close a project or phase correctly, including early termination.

## 7.1 What the Governance domain covers

The Governance domain addresses the **framework, functions and processes that guide project activities** so the project delivers its intended outcomes in line with organisational strategy, policies and obligations. It absorbs most of the former *Integration Management* knowledge area, plus quality assurance and the sourcing (procurement) strategy.

Good governance provides:

- **Clear decision rights** — who can decide what, and the thresholds (for example, the PM can approve schedule changes up to five days; the CCB beyond that; the steering committee for anything affecting the business case).
- **Accountability and oversight** — sponsor, steering committee, PMO and audits.
- **Escalation paths** — how issues, risks and decisions move up when they exceed authority.
- **Alignment** — the project stays linked to strategy and the business case through phase gates and reviews.
- **Integration** — plans, work and changes fit together as a coherent whole.
- **Compliance** — legal, regulatory, contractual and policy obligations are met (see Chapter 17).

**Signs of effective governance (desired outcomes):** decisions are timely and made at the right level; the project remains aligned with objectives; stakeholders trust the information they receive; changes are controlled without paralysing delivery; knowledge is captured and reused; the project or phase closes cleanly with value transitioned.

> **Exam tip.** Governance is **not** bureaucracy for its own sake. The best answer tailors governance to the context: lightweight and empowering for small adaptive teams, more formal for regulated, high-risk or contract-based work.

## 7.2 Initiate Project or Phase *(Initiating)*

**Purpose:** formally authorise the project (or the next phase), define its purpose and success criteria, and give the PM authority.

| Key inputs | Key tools & techniques | Key outputs |
|---|---|---|
| Business case, benefits management plan, agreements, EEFs, OPAs | Expert judgment, data gathering (brainstorming, focus groups, interviews), interpersonal skills (facilitation, conflict management, meeting management), meetings | **Project charter**, **assumption log** |

**Charter contents:** project purpose; measurable objectives and success criteria; high-level requirements; high-level description, boundaries and key deliverables; overall project risk; summary milestone schedule; pre-approved financial resources; key stakeholder list; project approval requirements (what constitutes success, who signs off); exit criteria; assigned PM, responsibility and authority level; name and authority of the sponsor.

**Assumption log:** records high-level strategic and operational assumptions and constraints identified at initiation; later refined with detailed assumptions.

**In adaptive contexts** the equivalent artifacts are a **product vision** (often an "elevator pitch"), a **project/product charter** or **team charter**, a **product roadmap** and an initial **product backlog**. Phase or release initiation re-confirms the vision and business value.

> **Trap.** The PM should be **assigned as early as possible** — ideally during charter development — but the PM never authorises their own project. The **sponsor** issues the charter.

## 7.3 Integrate and Align Project Plans *(Planning)*

**Purpose:** define, prepare and coordinate all plan components and consolidate them into an **integrated project management plan** aligned with governance, the development approach and stakeholder expectations.

- **Outputs:** the **project management plan** (subsidiary plans, baselines, change and configuration management plans, life cycle and development approach).
- **Techniques:** expert judgment, data gathering (checklists, brainstorming), interpersonal skills (conflict management, facilitation), meetings — especially the **kickoff meeting**.
- **Kickoff meeting:** marks the end of planning and the start of execution in predictive projects (small projects may hold it right after initiation). It communicates objectives, gains commitment, explains roles and responsibilities. In adaptive projects a similar launch (often called *iteration zero*, *chartering workshop* or *inception*) aligns the team on vision, ways of working and the initial backlog.
- **Progressive elaboration:** the plan is refined as the project progresses; once baselined, changes to it go through change control.
- **Plan delivery:** choose how the product will be delivered — releases, increments, milestones — and integrate adaptive release plans with predictive milestones in hybrid projects.

## 7.4 Plan Sourcing Strategy *(Planning)*

**Purpose:** decide **what to obtain from outside** the organisation, **how** (contract type, selection method) and **from whom**, and document it in the **sourcing strategy plan** (the Sixth Edition's procurement management plan). Appendix X4 (Procurement) expands on strategic implications, make-or-buy and the project manager's role.

### Make-or-buy analysis

Compare the cost, risk, capability, schedule and strategic implications of producing internally versus buying. Consider total cost (purchase price + indirect costs + life-cycle costs), core competence, intellectual property, confidentiality, capacity, and the effect on the team.

*Example:* Renting a machine costs $500/day; buying costs $18,000 plus $100/day to operate. Break-even: 18,000 + 100d = 500d → d = 45 days. If you need it for more than 45 days, buying is cheaper.

### Contract types

| Family | Type | How the seller is paid | Who carries the cost risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Fixed price (FP)** | **Firm fixed price (FFP)** | Fixed total price | **Seller** (most) |
| | **Fixed price incentive fee (FPIF)** | Fixed price + incentive for meeting targets; ceiling price; cost sharing ratio | Seller (shared up to the ceiling) |
| | **Fixed price with economic price adjustment (FP-EPA)** | Fixed price adjusted for defined changes (inflation, commodity prices) — for multi-year contracts | Seller (with protection against market changes) |
| **Cost-reimbursable (CR)** | **Cost plus fixed fee (CPFF)** | Allowable costs + fixed fee (usually % of *estimated* cost) | **Buyer** |
| | **Cost plus incentive fee (CPIF)** | Costs + fee that depends on performance against targets; cost overrun/underrun shared by ratio (e.g., 80/20) | Buyer (shared) |
| | **Cost plus award fee (CPAF)** | Costs + award fee based on the buyer's subjective evaluation of performance | Buyer |
| **Time and materials (T&M)** | — | Rate per hour/day + materials at cost; often a not-to-exceed limit | Buyer (mid-way between FP and CR) |

**How to choose:**
- **Scope well defined and stable** → fixed price.
- **Scope uncertain or evolving** → cost-reimbursable or T&M (with controls such as not-to-exceed caps).
- **Small amounts of staff augmentation, or work starting before a statement of work is complete** → T&M.
- **Buyer wants to motivate performance** → incentive or award fee.

**Agile contracting options** (from the *Agile Practice Guide*): fixed-price increments (per user story or sprint), not-to-exceed T&M, graduated fixed price (rate varies with early/late delivery), early cancellation option, dynamic scope option (swap features of equal size), team augmentation, multi-tiered structures (master agreement + statements of work).

### Source selection methods

Least cost; qualifications only; quality-based/highest technical score; **quality and cost-based** (weighted scoring); sole source (single supplier, with justification); fixed budget (scope adapted to a stated budget).

### Procurement documents

| Document | Use |
|---|---|
| **Request for information (RFI)** | Gather information on what sellers can offer — early market research |
| **Request for quotation (RFQ)** | Obtain prices for well-defined, standard items |
| **Request for proposal (RFP)** | Obtain proposals for complex work where sellers propose a solution |
| **Procurement statement of work (SOW)** | Describes the procured item in enough detail for sellers to determine if they can provide it (performance, functional or design SOW) |
| **Terms of reference (TOR)** | SOW equivalent for services |
| **Source selection criteria** | How proposals will be evaluated (technical approach, price, capability, past performance, financial stability, sustainability, IP rights) |
| **Independent cost estimate** | Buyer's own estimate to benchmark proposals |

> **Exam tip.** Procurement decisions are governed by **organisational policies and legal requirements**. The PM usually works *with* the procurement or contracts department, which holds the authority to sign contracts. A PM who promises a supplier the contract informally is creating legal and ethical problems.

## 7.5 Manage Project Execution *(Executing)*

**Purpose:** lead and perform the work defined in the project management plan, and implement **approved** changes, to achieve the project's objectives.

- **Outputs:** **deliverables**, **work performance data**, **issue log**, **change requests**, updates to plans and documents.
- **Approved change requests** (corrective actions, preventive actions, defect repairs) are *implemented* here.
- The **issue log** records current problems, owners, due dates and status.
- The PMIS and meetings support execution.

> **Trap.** A team member who implements a change the customer asked for — without approval — is creating **scope creep**. In a predictive context, the request must go through *Assess and Implement Changes*; in an adaptive context, it goes to the **product owner** for backlog prioritisation.

## 7.6 Manage Quality Assurance *(Executing)*

**Purpose:** translate the quality requirements and policies into executable quality activities and **confirm that the processes being used will produce deliverables that meet requirements**. Focus is on the *process* (assurance), whereas inspecting deliverables is quality *control* (done within *Monitor and Control Scope*).

Key techniques: **quality audits**, **process analysis**, checklists, **root-cause analysis**, design for X, problem solving, cause-and-effect diagrams, flowcharts, test and evaluation documents. Key outputs: **quality reports**, test and evaluation documents, change requests (process improvements). Details in Chapter 9.

## 7.7 Manage Project Knowledge *(Executing)*

**Purpose:** use existing knowledge and create new knowledge to achieve objectives and contribute to organisational learning.

| Explicit knowledge | Tacit knowledge |
|---|---|
| Easily codified in words, pictures, numbers (manuals, lessons learned, procedures) | Personal, hard to express (experience, insights, "know-how") |
| Shared through documents and repositories (information management) | Shared through **conversation and interaction**: communities of practice, mentoring, job shadowing, storytelling, workshops, pairing, knowledge fairs |

- **Key output:** **lessons learned register** — created early and updated *throughout* the project (not only at the end). At closure it is transferred to the organisational **lessons learned repository** (an OPA).
- **Knowledge transfer** to operations and to future projects is an explicit PMP task (People domain, task 7).
- In agile teams, retrospectives, pairing, mob programming and shared code ownership spread knowledge continuously.

## 7.8 Monitor and Control Project Performance *(Monitoring and Controlling)*

**Purpose:** track, review and report overall progress toward the objectives defined in the plan, and recommend corrective or preventive actions.

- Combines work performance information from every control process (scope, schedule, finance, resources, risk, stakeholders, communications).
- Techniques: **variance analysis**, **trend analysis**, **earned value analysis**, root-cause analysis, alternatives analysis, cost-benefit analysis, decision-making, meetings.
- Outputs: **work performance reports** (status reports, dashboards, forecasts), change requests.
- Agile/hybrid equivalents: burnup/burndown charts, cumulative flow diagrams, velocity trends, information radiators, sprint reviews.

## 7.9 Assess and Implement Changes *(Monitoring and Controlling)*

**Purpose:** review all change requests; approve, defer or reject them; manage changes to deliverables, project documents and the project management plan; and communicate decisions. This is **integrated change control**.

### The change control flow (predictive)

1. **Identify** the need for a change (anyone can raise it).
2. **Document** it in a change request (written; verbal requests are captured in writing).
3. **Evaluate the impact** on scope, schedule, cost, quality, risk, resources, stakeholders and the business case — *before* deciding (holistic view).
4. **Explore options** (alternatives, trade-offs).
5. **Decide** — by the person or body with the authority (PM within thresholds, CCB, sponsor, steering committee, customer for contract changes).
6. **Update** the plan, baselines and documents; record in the **change log**.
7. **Communicate** the decision to affected stakeholders.
8. **Implement** (in Manage Project Execution) and **verify** the change was implemented correctly.

**Configuration management** supports change control by managing the *specifications of the product and documents*: **configuration identification** (what items are controlled), **configuration status accounting** (recording and reporting their state), and **configuration verification and audit** (confirming the composition is correct and changes were implemented as approved).

### Change in adaptive contexts

- Change is expected and welcomed. New ideas and changed requirements become **backlog items** that the **product owner** prioritises; the *current* sprint goal is protected (the team and PO negotiate if something truly urgent arises).
- Changes to the **overall** constraints (release date, budget, contract, fundamental vision) still follow governance, and may need sponsor or steering approval.

> **Exam tip — the classic sequence for a change request in a predictive project:** *evaluate impact → explore options → submit to CCB → update plan and log → communicate → implement.* The wrong answers usually skip impact analysis, go straight to the sponsor, implement before approval, or refuse to consider the change at all.

## 7.10 Close Project or Phase *(Closing)*

**Purpose:** finalise all activities for the project, phase or contract; archive information; release resources; and ensure value is transitioned.

**Closing activities:**
- Confirm all work is completed and deliverables are **formally accepted** (accepted deliverables come from *Validate Scope*).
- **Transfer** the final product, service or result to operations or the customer (with training, documentation, support arrangements).
- **Close procurements**: verify all contract work, settle claims, make final payments, issue formal written notice of contract completion, archive procurement records.
- Measure stakeholder satisfaction; complete the **final report** (summary of performance against objectives: scope, quality, cost, schedule, benefits, risks/issues).
- Finalise and transfer **lessons learned**; update OPAs; **archive** project documents.
- **Release resources** (team and physical) and celebrate/recognise contributions.
- Hand over **benefit tracking** to the benefit owner.

**Early termination:** if the project is cancelled, the PM still performs closure: document the reason, the status of deliverables (completed and incomplete), transfer partial work, close contracts per their termination clauses, capture lessons learned, release resources.

> **Trap.** "The customer has verbally accepted the product; release the team" — wrong. Obtain **formal written acceptance**, complete administrative closure and lessons learned **before** releasing resources.

## 7.11 Governance in adaptive and hybrid environments

- **Lightweight, value-based governance:** funding by increment or by product (rather than one big up-front approval), with continuation decisions based on demonstrated value.
- **Guardrails instead of gates:** clear boundaries (budget, compliance rules, architecture standards) within which teams decide.
- **Frequent transparency:** sprint reviews and information radiators replace some formal status reports.
- **Hybrid governance:** phase gates for investment decisions; sprints inside phases; a single integrated view of progress and risk for the steering committee.
- **The PMO** may evolve into a **value delivery office (VDO)** or agile centre of excellence that coaches teams rather than policing them (Appendix X2; see Chapter 17).

@@
A project's scope is unclear, and the buyer wants the seller to start immediately while requirements are being developed. Which contract type is most appropriate?
A) Time and materials with a not-to-exceed limit
B) Firm fixed price
C) Fixed price with economic price adjustment
D) Fixed price incentive fee with a ceiling price
= A
? When scope cannot yet be defined but work must start, T&M (ideally capped) or cost-reimbursable contracts are appropriate. Fixed-price contracts require a well-defined scope.

@@
Under which contract type does the seller bear the greatest cost risk?
A) Firm fixed price
B) Cost plus fixed fee
C) Cost plus award fee
D) Time and materials
= A
? In an FFP contract the seller must deliver for the agreed price regardless of actual costs. Cost-reimbursable contracts shift most risk to the buyer, and T&M sits in between.

@@
A customer emails a developer directly asking for an extra report. The developer adds it without telling anyone. In a predictive project, what does this represent and what should have happened?
A) Scope creep; it should go through integrated change control
B) Gold plating; the developer should be disciplined immediately for agreeing to it
C) Good customer service that should be encouraged
D) A defect repair that needs no approval
= A
? Uncontrolled additions requested by the customer are scope creep. Gold plating is when the team adds extras on its own initiative. The request should be captured as a change request and evaluated before implementation.

@@
A change request has been submitted to alter a key deliverable in a predictive project. What should the project manager do first?
A) Evaluate its impact on scope, schedule, cost, quality, risk and value
B) Submit it directly to the change control board without any analysis, to save time
C) Implement it to keep the customer satisfied
D) Reject it because the scope baseline is approved
= A
? Before a decision, the PM analyses the impact and options so the decision-maker can make an informed choice. Implementing before approval and rejecting without analysis are both wrong.

What is the main difference between explicit and tacit knowledge?

B) Tacit knowledge is always written down in the lessons learned register, while explicit knowledge is not
C) Explicit knowledge cannot be shared outside the team
D) There is no difference; both terms describe documentation
= A
? Explicit knowledge is easily written down; tacit knowledge such as experience and judgment is transferred through conversation, observation and collaboration.

B) Only during the final closure meeting, when the whole team can reflect on the project
