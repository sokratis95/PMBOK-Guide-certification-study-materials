## 17.1 Compliance (Business Environment task 2)

**Compliance** = meeting the requirements imposed by laws, regulations, standards, contracts and organisational policies. Examples: data protection (e.g., GDPR), health and safety, environmental permits, accessibility, financial controls (e.g., SOX), industry standards (ISO, GMP in pharma, aviation regulations), labour law, anti-bribery and corruption laws, export controls, sustainability reporting rules.

**Managing compliance:**
1. **Identify** compliance requirements early (legal, regulatory, contractual, policy) with experts — legal, compliance, quality, security.
2. **Classify** them by category and risk (what happens if we fail?).
3. **Plan** how compliance will be achieved and demonstrated — build requirements into scope, acceptance criteria, the definition of done, the quality plan and the schedule (e.g., audit dates, regulatory submissions).
4. **Assign owners** and integrate into governance (compliance checkpoints at phase gates).
5. **Monitor and audit** — compliance audits, inspections, evidence collection.
6. **Analyse the consequences of non-compliance** (fines, shutdown, reputational damage, safety incidents) and treat them as risks.
7. **Address non-compliance immediately** — stop the non-compliant activity if needed, inform the right authorities internally, correct, and prevent recurrence.

> **Exam tip.** Compliance is **not negotiable** and not something to "trade off" for schedule. If a regulation changes during the project, assess the impact and process the needed changes — don't ignore it, and don't wait until the end. If you discover a violation, **don't hide it**: report it through the proper channels.

## 17.2 Issues and impediments (Business Environment task 4)

| | **Risk** | **Issue** | **Impediment (blocker)** |
|---|---|---|---|
| Definition | Uncertain future event | Current problem that must be resolved | Anything that slows or blocks the team's progress |
| Where recorded | Risk register | **Issue log** | Impediment board / issue log; raised in daily stand-ups |
| Typical owner | Risk owner | Issue owner (assigned) | Scrum master / servant leader, or whoever can remove it |

**Issue log** contents: issue type, who raised it and when, description, priority, **owner**, target resolution date, status, final solution.

**Handling issues and impediments:**
1. Make them **visible** (issue log, impediment board, stand-ups).
2. **Analyse** the root cause and impact.
3. **Resolve at the lowest appropriate level** — the team first, then the PM/scrum master.
4. **Escalate** through the agreed path when the resolution is outside the team's authority (functional manager, sponsor, steering committee) — with facts and options.
5. **Track** to closure and capture lessons learned; identify related risks.

> **Trap.** The servant leader removes impediments **the team cannot remove itself** — e.g., getting access to a test environment, resolving a dependency with another department. Solving every problem *for* a self-organising team (or ignoring impediments) are both wrong.

## 17.3 Benefits and value realisation

- **Benefits management plan** (Chapter 3): target benefits, owner, metrics, timeframe, assumptions, risks.
- **Value-based delivery (Process task 3)**: identify value components with stakeholders; **prioritise by value** (MoSCoW, WSJF, cost of delay); **deliver incrementally** (MVP, releases); **measure** benefits with KPIs and **OKRs** (objectives and key results); adjust based on feedback.
- **Leading vs lagging indicators**: leading indicators predict future results (e.g., feature adoption, pipeline of sales demos); lagging indicators confirm results after the fact (e.g., revenue, customer churn).
- **Transition to operations**: training, documentation, support model, service-level agreements, operational readiness reviews, hypercare period, handover of benefit tracking.
- Benefits are often realised **after** the project closes — the benefit owner continues measuring them.

## 17.4 Supporting organisational change (Business Environment task 7)

Projects create change; people must adopt it for benefits to occur. The PM supports **organisational change management (OCM)** — often with change managers and business leaders.

### Change models

| Model | Stages / elements |
|---|---|
| **Lewin** | **Unfreeze** (create readiness) → **Change** (move) → **Refreeze** (stabilise in new state) |
| **Kotter's 8 steps** | 1 Create urgency · 2 Form a powerful guiding coalition · 3 Create a vision for change · 4 Communicate the vision · 5 Remove obstacles (empower action) · 6 Create short-term wins · 7 Build on the change (consolidate) · 8 Anchor the changes in corporate culture |
| **ADKAR® (Prosci)** | **A**wareness of the need for change · **D**esire to participate · **K**nowledge of how to change · **A**bility to implement · **R**einforcement to sustain |
| **Bridges' transition model** | **Ending, losing, letting go** → **Neutral zone** → **New beginning** (focuses on people's psychological transition, not the change itself) |
| **Virginia Satir change model** | Late status quo → Introduction of a foreign element → **Chaos** → Transforming idea → **Practice and integration** → New status quo (performance dips before improving) |
| **Diffusion of innovations (Rogers)** | Innovators, early adopters, early majority, late majority, laggards — target early adopters as champions |

### Practical actions

- **Assess readiness** (culture, capacity for change, past change experiences, leadership support).
- **Engage stakeholders** early; identify **champions/change agents**; involve users in design.
- **Communicate** the *why*, *what* and *what's in it for me* — repeatedly, in multiple channels.
- **Train and support** (coaching, super-users, help desks).
- Expect and **address resistance** — listen to concerns; resistance often signals legitimate issues.
- **Celebrate quick wins**; **reinforce** with metrics, recognition, updated processes and policies.
- Avoid **change saturation** — too many simultaneous changes for the same people.

## 17.5 Evaluating external business environment changes (Business Environment task 8)

External changes — **regulatory**, **economic** (inflation, interest rates, exchange rates, tariffs), **market** (competitors, customer preferences), **technological** (new platforms, AI), **social** and **environmental** — can alter the project's value, scope, risks and constraints.

**Approach:**
1. **Scan** the environment continuously (PESTLE, market research, regulatory watch, supplier news).
2. **Assess the impact** on the business case, scope, schedule, budget, risks, compliance.
3. **Recommend options** (continue, adapt scope, re-plan, pause, cancel) and involve the decision-makers (sponsor, steering committee, product owner).
4. **Process changes** through governance (change requests, backlog reprioritisation, re-baselining).
5. **Communicate** changes and update the risk register, assumption log and business case.

## 17.6 Continuous improvement (Business Environment task 6)

- **Lessons learned** — captured throughout and shared (Chapter 7).
- **Retrospectives** — every iteration; produce **actionable** improvements that are tracked (often added to the backlog).
- **PDCA / Kaizen** — small, frequent improvements; **Six Sigma DMAIC** for process problems; **Lean** waste elimination; **value stream mapping**.
- **Root-cause analysis** (5 Whys, fishbone) — fix causes, not symptoms.
- **Experiments** — try a change for a limited time, measure, keep or discard.
- **Update OPAs** — templates, checklists, processes — so the organisation improves, not just the project.
- **Process audits** and **maturity models** (e.g., OPM3, CMMI) at organisational level.

## 17.7 Sustainability and ESG

The principle *Integrate sustainability within all project areas* (Chapter 4) plays out in the business environment as:

- **Triple bottom line** — people, planet, prosperity.
- **ESG** — environmental (emissions, energy, waste, water, biodiversity), social (labour practices, health and safety, community impact, diversity and inclusion, human rights in the supply chain), governance (ethics, transparency, anti-corruption, board oversight).
- **UN Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs)** — 17 global goals many organisations align to.
- **Sustainable procurement** — supplier codes of conduct, environmental and social criteria in source selection, local sourcing.
- **Life-cycle thinking** — design for durability, repair, reuse, recycling (**circular economy**); **life cycle assessment (LCA)** of environmental impact.
- **Carbon footprint** — measure and reduce project and product emissions (scope 1, 2, 3 emissions in corporate reporting).
- **Regulatory sustainability reporting** — organisations may be legally required to report sustainability data; projects may need to provide it.
- **Social sustainability of the team** — sustainable pace, well-being, fair treatment.

## 17.8 Artificial intelligence in project management (Appendix X3)

The Eighth Edition adds an appendix on AI. Key ideas:

**Common use cases**
- **Planning and scheduling** — draft WBS/backlogs, generate schedule options, estimate from historical data, optimise resources.
- **Risk management** — identify risks from documents and history, predict delays or overruns, analyse sentiment in communications.
- **Reporting and communications** — summarise meetings, draft status reports, translate, tailor messages for audiences.
- **Analytics and forecasting** — predictive analytics on cost and schedule, anomaly detection, scenario simulation.
- **Knowledge management** — search lessons learned, answer questions from project documents.
- **Administrative automation** — meeting notes, action tracking, document drafting, procurement document analysis.

**Responsible and ethical use**
- **Human oversight and accountability** — AI supports decisions; **people remain accountable** for them (principle: *Be an accountable leader*). Validate outputs before use.
- **Transparency** — disclose when content or decisions are AI-assisted; explainability of models.
- **Data privacy and confidentiality** — don't feed confidential, personal or client data into tools that are not approved; follow data protection laws and company policy.
- **Bias and fairness** — AI can replicate biases in its training data (e.g., in resource selection); check for fairness.
- **Accuracy** — AI can generate plausible but wrong outputs ("hallucinations"); verify facts and calculations.
- **Intellectual property** — respect copyright and licensing; clarify ownership of AI-generated content.
- **Security** — approved tools, access controls.
- **Sustainability** — consider the energy use of AI.

**Adopting AI**
- Start with a clear **purpose and value** (use cases with measurable benefits); assess **data readiness and quality**; run **pilots**; establish **AI governance** (policies, approved tools, roles); **train** people (AI literacy); manage the **organisational change** (fears about jobs, trust); monitor results and **improve**.

**AI projects themselves** have particular characteristics: experimental, data-dependent, iterative model development, uncertain outcomes (adaptive approaches fit well), plus ethical and regulatory risks.

> **Exam tip.** In AI scenarios, the best answer usually **uses AI to augment** the team while keeping **human judgment, transparency, data protection and compliance** — e.g., "validate the AI-generated schedule with the team before baselining", "use only the organisation's approved AI tools for confidential data".

## 17.9 The Project Management Office (Appendix X2)

A **PMO** is an organisational structure that standardises project-related governance processes and facilitates the sharing of resources, methodologies, tools and techniques.

| Type | Degree of control | Role |
|---|---|---|
| **Supportive** | **Low** | Consultative: templates, best practices, training, access to lessons learned |
| **Controlling** | **Moderate** | Support **and** require compliance (frameworks, methodologies, templates, governance reviews) |
| **Directive** | **High** | Directly **manages** the projects; project managers report to the PMO |

Other forms: **enterprise PMO (EPMO)** aligning portfolio to strategy; **program management office**; **agile PMO / value delivery office (VDO)** that coaches teams, supports agile practices and focuses on value rather than compliance; **centre of excellence**.

**PMO value proposition:** better alignment with strategy, consistent governance, improved decision-making (portfolio visibility), capability development, resource optimisation, knowledge management, and **customer focus** — a PMO must demonstrate value to its customers (executives, PMs, teams) and evolve, otherwise it is seen as bureaucracy. PMO **maturity models** help assess and improve capabilities.
