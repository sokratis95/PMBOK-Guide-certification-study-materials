# Chapter 3 — The Standard: A System for Value Delivery, Governance and the Project Environment

**After this chapter you should be able to:**
- explain how portfolios, programs, projects, products and operations work together to create value;
- distinguish organisational governance from project governance and describe the main governance roles;
- classify internal and external environmental factors and organisational process assets;
- compare organisational structures and their effect on the project manager's authority;
- describe the business documents that justify and authorise a project (needs assessment, business case, benefits management plan, charter);
- describe the project manager's role and the PMI Talent Triangle.

## 3.1 Value is the reason projects exist

A project is not successful just because it delivered on time and on budget. It is successful when it contributes to **value** — the worth, importance or usefulness that stakeholders obtain. Value can be:

- **tangible**: revenue, cost savings, market share, assets, equity;
- **intangible**: reputation, brand recognition, public benefit, strategic alignment, employee goodwill, sustainability.

The chain that connects work to value is:

**Activities → Outputs (deliverables) → Outcomes (changes in behaviour or capability) → Benefits (gains for the organisation) → Value**

> **Exam tip.** When a question asks how to measure success, prefer answers that refer to **outcomes, benefits and value** (customer adoption, business KPIs, benefit realisation) over answers that refer only to outputs or to the triple constraint.

## 3.2 The system for value delivery

Organisations do not deliver value through isolated projects. They use a **system for value delivery**: a collection of strategic business activities aimed at building, sustaining and advancing the organisation. Its components are:

| Component | Role in value delivery |
|---|---|
| **Portfolios** | Select and prioritise the right work to execute strategy; balance investments and risks |
| **Programs** | Coordinate related projects and activities to realise benefits that would not be obtained individually |
| **Projects** | Create the outputs that enable outcomes and benefits |
| **Products** | Deliver value continuously over their life cycle; may be created or changed by many projects |
| **Operations** | Run the business and *sustain* the value once deliverables are transitioned |

**Information flows in both directions.** Strategy, priorities and constraints flow *down* from leadership through portfolios and programs to projects. Performance data, lessons, risks and benefit realisation information flow *up*, so leadership can adjust strategy and investments. A project manager who reports only "green" status is breaking this feedback loop.

### Value is delivered continuously

In adaptive and product-oriented organisations, value is delivered in increments: each release is used, measured and fed back into the backlog. Value delivery does not wait for project closure. Predictive projects may deliver most value at the end, but benefits are still tracked after the transition to operations.

### Products and product management

The Eighth Edition gives explicit attention to **product management considerations**:

- A **product life cycle** (introduction, growth, maturity, decline, retirement) is longer than any single project.
- Organisations increasingly fund **long-lived product teams** rather than temporary project teams ("project-to-product" shift).
- Projects may create a new product, add capabilities, or retire one.
- A product focus emphasises customer outcomes, continuous discovery, roadmaps and backlog management — vocabulary you will meet again in Chapter 15.

## 3.3 Governance: organisational and project

**Governance** is the framework of authority, accountability, policies, decision rights and oversight that directs and controls work. Two levels matter:

| Level | Examples | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| **Organisational governance** | Board, executive committees, portfolio governance, policies, compliance rules | Sets strategy, risk appetite and rules for the whole organisation |
| **Project governance** | Sponsor, steering committee, phase gates, change control board, escalation paths, PMO oversight | Guides and oversees the specific project within the organisation's framework |

A good governance framework answers: *Who decides what? Within what limits (thresholds)? How are issues escalated? How is performance reviewed? How are changes approved? How are compliance and ethics ensured?*

### Key governance roles

| Role | Typical responsibilities |
|---|---|
| **Sponsor** | Champions the project, provides funding and resources, approves the charter, makes decisions above the PM's authority, removes organisational obstacles, is usually accountable for benefits |
| **Steering committee / project board** | Provides strategic direction, approves major changes and phase gates, resolves escalated issues |
| **Portfolio manager / portfolio board** | Prioritises investments, allocates scarce resources across projects, decides on start/stop |
| **Program manager** | Coordinates related projects, manages interdependencies and benefits |
| **PMO** | Standardises governance, provides methods, templates, training, reporting and sometimes direct management of projects |
| **Change control board (CCB)** | Reviews, approves, defers or rejects change requests according to authority thresholds |
| **Product owner** (adaptive) | Owns the product backlog, maximises value, decides priorities and accepts work |
| **Project manager** | Leads the team to achieve objectives, integrates the work, operates within delegated authority |
| **Functional / resource managers** | Provide people and expertise, often hold administrative authority over team members |

## 3.4 Functions needed in every project

Regardless of job titles, every project needs certain **functions** to be fulfilled. One person may perform several functions and one function may be shared:

- **Provide oversight and coordination** — plan, monitor, integrate (often the PM, PMO or scrum master/team facilitator).
- **Present objectives and feedback** — articulate needs, priorities and acceptance (customer, product owner, users).
- **Facilitate and support** — encourage participation, consensus, removing impediments (PM, scrum master, coach).
- **Perform work and contribute insights** — create the deliverables (team members, contractors).
- **Apply expertise** — subject-matter experts, specialists.
- **Provide business direction and insight** — clarify value and priorities (sponsor, product owner, business analyst).
- **Provide resources and direction** — sponsor, functional managers, governance bodies.
- **Maintain governance** — approve, oversee and ensure compliance (steering committee, PMO, CCB).

## 3.5 The project environment

Projects exist inside an environment that influences them. The Guide distinguishes **internal** and **external** factors.

| Internal environment | External environment |
|---|---|
| Organisational culture, structure and governance | Marketplace conditions (competitors, market share, brand) |
| Geographic distribution of facilities and people | Social and cultural influences, political climate, ethics |
| Infrastructure, IT systems and software | Regulatory and legal environment (laws, standards, data protection, safety) |
| Resource availability and employee capability | Commercial databases and benchmarking data |
| Process assets (templates, procedures, policies) | Academic research and industry standards |
| Data assets and knowledge assets (lessons learned, historical data) | Financial considerations (exchange rates, interest rates, inflation, tariffs) |
| Security and safety policies | Physical environment (weather, working conditions) |

Two traditional labels are still widely used in exam questions:

- **Enterprise environmental factors (EEFs)**: conditions *not under the team's immediate control* (internal or external) that influence, constrain or direct the project.
- **Organisational process assets (OPAs)**: plans, processes, policies, procedures and knowledge bases *of the performing organisation*. They are of two kinds: **processes, policies and procedures** (usually not updated by the project) and **organisational knowledge repositories** (updated by the project — e.g., lessons learned, historical information).

> **Trap.** An organisation's *culture* is an EEF, not an OPA, even though it is internal. Templates and lessons learned are OPAs.

## 3.6 Organisational structures and the project manager's authority

| Structure | PM authority | Resource availability | Who controls the budget | PM role |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Organic / simple** | Little or none | Little or none | Owner/operator | Part-time, coordinator |
| **Functional (centralised)** | Little or none | Little or none | Functional manager | Part-time; often called *project coordinator* or *expediter* |
| **Multi-divisional** | Little or none | Little or none | Functional manager | Part-time |
| **Weak matrix** | Low | Low | Functional manager | Part-time; coordinator/expediter |
| **Balanced matrix** | Low to moderate | Low to moderate | Mixed | Full-time |
| **Strong matrix** | Moderate to high | Moderate to high | Project manager | Full-time, with admin staff |
| **Project-oriented (projectized)** | High to almost total | High to almost total | Project manager | Full-time |
| **Virtual / networked** | Low to moderate | Low to moderate | Mixed | Full- or part-time |
| **Hybrid** | Mixed | Mixed | Mixed | Mixed |
| **PMO-led** | High | High | Project manager | Full-time |

- A **project expediter** acts as a staff assistant and communications coordinator with no decision authority.
- A **project coordinator** has some limited authority and usually reports to a higher-level manager.
- In a **matrix**, team members have two bosses: the functional manager (administrative, career, often technical) and the project manager (project work). Conflicts over priorities and resources are common — the PM must **negotiate** and use influence rather than formal power.

## 3.7 Business documents: justifying and authorising the project

| Document | Owner | Content | Purpose |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Needs assessment** | Business / business analyst | Problem or opportunity, current state, desired future state, gaps | Understand the business need before investing |
| **Business case** | Sponsor (prepared with business analysts) | Business need, situation analysis, options (do nothing, do minimum, do more), recommended option, cost-benefit analysis, financial measures (NPV, IRR, payback), risks, success criteria | Justify the investment; reviewed at phase gates to confirm the project is still worthwhile |
| **Benefits management plan** | Sponsor / benefit owner | Target benefits, strategic alignment, timeframe for realisation, **benefit owner**, metrics, assumptions, risks | Describe how and when benefits will be delivered and measured |
| **Project charter** | Issued by the **sponsor** (or initiator); developed with the PM | Purpose, measurable objectives and success criteria, high-level requirements, boundaries and key deliverables, overall risk, summary milestones, pre-approved financial resources, key stakeholders, approval and exit criteria, **assigned PM and authority level**, sponsor's name and authority | **Formally authorises the project** and gives the PM authority to apply organisational resources |

> **Exam tip.** The **charter** authorises the project; the **business case** justifies it; the **benefits management plan** describes how value will be realised and measured. If a question asks "the project no longer seems worthwhile — what should be reviewed?", the answer is usually the **business case** (and the benefits plan), often at a phase gate.

### Agreements and contracts

When the project is performed for an external customer, an **agreement** (contract, memorandum of understanding, letter of intent, service-level agreement) defines the initial intentions and is an input to initiating the project.

## 3.8 The project manager's role and competencies

The project manager is the person assigned by the performing organisation to lead the team responsible for achieving the project objectives. A common metaphor is the **conductor of an orchestra**: the PM does not play every instrument but integrates and directs. The PM's spheres of influence include the **project** (team, sponsor, stakeholders), the **organisation** (other PMs, PMO, executives), the **industry** (trends, standards, regulations) and the **profession** (knowledge transfer, continuing education, mentoring).

### The PMI Talent Triangle®

| Side | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Ways of Working** | Mastery of predictive, agile, hybrid, design thinking and other approaches; selecting and tailoring methods |
| **Power Skills** | Collaborative leadership, communication, innovative mindset, for-purpose orientation, empathy, negotiation, problem-solving |
| **Business Acumen** | Understanding of the organisation's strategy, finance, markets, competition, regulations; linking the project to value |

> **What changed.** Earlier versions of the Talent Triangle used *Technical Project Management*, *Leadership* and *Strategic and Business Management*. You may still see these labels in older materials.

### Integration — the PM's core job

Integration happens at several levels: **process** (making the pieces of the plan fit together), **cognitive** (applying knowledge and judgment), **context** (adapting to the environment and technology) and **complexity** (managing the system behaviour of the project). The PM, not the team, is ultimately accountable for integrating the project as a whole.

%%QUESTIONS%%
@@
Which document formally authorises the existence of a project and gives the project manager authority to apply organisational resources?
A) Project charter
B) Business case
C) Benefits management plan
D) Project management plan
= A
? The charter, issued by the sponsor or initiator, formally authorises the project and documents the PM's authority. The business case justifies the investment and the benefits plan describes how benefits will be realised.

@@
Midway through a predictive project, a competitor launches a product that makes the planned solution far less attractive. At the next phase gate, which document should the steering committee primarily revisit to decide whether to continue?
A) The business case
B) The project schedule
C) The resource calendar
D) The communications management plan
= A
? The business case contains the justification and cost-benefit analysis for the investment. Phase gates are the natural point to confirm it is still valid and decide to continue, change or stop the project.

@@
Who is typically accountable for the realisation of benefits after a project's deliverables are transferred to operations?
A) The sponsor or the designated benefit owner
B) The project manager, who delivers the outputs
C) The project team, which builds the deliverables
D) The procurement department
= A
? The benefits management plan names a benefit owner, often the sponsor or an operational manager, who is accountable for realising benefits after transition. The PM's accountability normally ends at project closure.

@@
In which organisational structure does the project manager have the least authority and usually acts as a coordinator or expediter?
A) Functional
B) Strong matrix
C) Projectized
D) PMO-led
= A
? In a functional structure the functional manager controls resources and budget; the PM role is part-time with little or no authority.

@@
A project manager in a balanced matrix organisation needs a senior engineer who reports to a functional manager. What is the best approach?
A) Negotiate with the functional manager for the engineer's time
B) Assign the engineer directly, because the project has an approved charter and budget
C) Escalate immediately to the CEO
D) Hire a contractor without consulting anyone
= A
? In a matrix the functional manager controls the resource, so the PM must negotiate and use influence. Escalation is reserved for when negotiation fails; bypassing the manager breaks governance.

@@
Which of the following is classified as an enterprise environmental factor?
A) The organisation's culture and appetite for risk
B) The company's project schedule template
C) The lessons learned repository
D) The organisation's documented change control procedure for projects
= A
? Culture and risk appetite influence the project but are not under the team's immediate control, so they are EEFs. Templates, repositories and procedures are organisational process assets.

@@
Why is information expected to flow both up and down the system for value delivery?
A) Strategy guides the work, and performance and benefit information lets leaders adjust strategy
B) So the project manager can bypass the sponsor when needed and report directly to the board of directors
C) So that operations can approve project change requests
D) Because portfolios report to projects
= A
? Leadership sets direction downward; projects and operations feed back performance, lessons and benefit data upward. Without honest upward feedback, the organisation cannot rebalance its portfolio.

@@
An organisation funds a permanent cross-functional team to evolve its mobile banking app over several years. This reflects which concept?
A) A product-oriented approach that delivers value continuously
B) A projectized organisational structure
C) A temporary project with a fixed end date and a dedicated, cross-functional team
D) A portfolio of unrelated projects
= A
? Long-lived product teams that deliver value continuously reflect product management considerations described in the Standard, distinct from temporary project teams.

Which side of the PMI Talent Triangle includes negotiation, communication and collaborative leadership?
A) Power Skills
B) Ways of Working
C) Business Acumen
D) Technical Project Management
= A
? Power Skills are the interpersonal skills; Ways of Working covers delivery approaches and techniques; Business Acumen covers strategy, finance and market understanding.

@@
A sponsor asks the project manager to prepare the charter. What is the most appropriate response?
A) Develop it with the sponsor, who issues and signs it
B) Refuse, because only the PMO may write charters
C) Write and sign the charter alone, then start planning
D) Skip the charter because a business case already exists
= A
? The PM often helps develop the charter, but it is issued by the sponsor or initiator at a level appropriate to fund and authorise the project. The PM cannot authorise their own project.

@@
In the system for value delivery, which component sustains the value of deliverables after a project ends?
A) Operations
B) The PMO
C) The change control board
D) The project team
= A
? Once deliverables are transitioned, operations run and sustain them, enabling benefits over time. Projects create the outputs; operations keep them working.
