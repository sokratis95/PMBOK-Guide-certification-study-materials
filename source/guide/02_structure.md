# Chapter 2 — PMBOK® Guide 8 at a Glance: Structure, Evolution and Core Vocabulary

**After this chapter you should be able to:**
- describe how the Eighth Edition is organised and why it changed;
- explain the "why – what – how" logic of principles, performance domains and processes;
- define the core terms used everywhere in the exam (project, program, portfolio, operations, value, outcome, benefit…);
- translate older knowledge-area vocabulary into Eighth-Edition terms.

## 2.1 What the PMBOK® Guide is

The *PMBOK® Guide – Eighth Edition* (published by PMI in late 2025) is a single volume containing two parts:

1. **The Standard for Project Management** — a short, consensus-based (ANSI-accredited) description of *what* project management is: the system for value delivery, the project environment and the **six principles** that guide behaviour.
2. **A Guide to the Project Management Body of Knowledge** — the larger part, describing the **seven performance domains**, the **40 processes** grouped in **five focus areas**, **tailoring**, and the **models, methods and artifacts** practitioners use.

It closes with appendices. Those most relevant to the exam are **X2 – Project Management Office (PMO)**, **X3 – Artificial Intelligence (AI)** and **X4 – Procurement**, plus a glossary. (X1 lists contributors and X5 describes the evolution of the Guide.)

> **Exam tip.** The PMBOK® Guide is *descriptive and non-prescriptive*: it describes good practice that is applicable to most projects most of the time, and the project team **tailors** it. An answer that says "always follow every process in the Guide" is suspicious.

## 2.2 How we got here — the evolution of the Guide

| Edition | Year | Organising idea |
|---|---|---|
| 1st–5th | 1996–2013 | Process-based: process groups × knowledge areas, inputs/tools & techniques/outputs (ITTOs) |
| **6th** | 2017 | **5 process groups, 10 knowledge areas, 49 processes**; agile content added in each knowledge area; *Agile Practice Guide* bundled |
| **7th** | 2021 | Radical shift to **principles**: **12 principles**, **8 performance domains**, tailoring, **models, methods and artifacts**; processes moved to PMIstandards+ |
| **8th** | 2025 | Synthesis: **6 principles, 7 performance domains, 5 focus areas, 40 processes**, tailoring, models/methods/artifacts, new appendices on PMO, AI and procurement |

Why the change? Practitioners found the Sixth Edition too process-heavy for adaptive work, and the Seventh Edition too abstract to apply ("we know the principles, but *how* do we do it?"). The Eighth Edition keeps the principles-and-domains foundation of the Seventh Edition, simplifies it, and **reintroduces processes** — but as non-prescriptive guidance that applies to predictive, adaptive and hybrid work alike.

### The three layers: why, what and how

| Layer | Eighth-Edition element | Question it answers |
|---|---|---|
| **Why** | **6 principles** | What beliefs and behaviours should guide every decision? |
| **What** | **7 performance domains** | Which areas of activity must be managed effectively for the project to deliver its outcomes? |
| **How** | **40 processes in 5 focus areas** + tailoring + models, methods and artifacts | What do we actually do, in which order, with which tools? |

### The six principles (Chapter 4)

1. **Adopt a holistic view**
2. **Focus on value**
3. **Embed quality into processes and deliverables**
4. **Be an accountable leader**
5. **Integrate sustainability within all project areas**
6. **Build an empowered culture**

### The seven performance domains (Chapters 7–14)

| Domain | Main concern | Number of processes |
|---|---|---|
| **Governance** | Decision structures, oversight, integration of plans, execution, quality assurance, knowledge, change, closure | 9 |
| **Scope** | Requirements, scope definition and structure, validation and control (including quality control) | 6 |
| **Schedule** | Planning, developing and controlling the schedule | 3 |
| **Finance** | Financial planning, cost estimates, budget, financial control and funding | 4 |
| **Stakeholders** | Identification, engagement and communications | 7 |
| **Resources** | Team and physical/virtual resources, acquisition, leadership, resource control | 5 |
| **Risk** | Risk planning, identification, analysis, responses and monitoring | 6 |
| | **Total** | **40** |

### The five focus areas (Chapter 6)

**Initiating → Planning → Executing → Monitoring and Controlling → Closing.** These are the former *process groups*. They describe the *nature* of the work, not phases in time; they repeat in every phase, release or iteration.

## 2.3 What happened to the ten knowledge areas?

If you studied with Sixth-Edition material, this translation table will save you time:

| Sixth Edition knowledge area | Where it lives in the Eighth Edition |
|---|---|
| Integration Management | **Governance** domain (charter, integrated plan, execution, knowledge, monitoring, change control, closure) |
| Scope Management | **Scope** domain |
| Schedule Management | **Schedule** domain (activity definition, sequencing and estimating are folded into *Develop Schedule*) |
| Cost Management | **Finance** domain (broadened to funding and financial value) |
| Quality Management | Spread out: quality **planning** inside *Define Scope*, quality **assurance** in Governance (*Manage Quality Assurance*), quality **control** inside *Monitor and Control Scope* — reflecting the principle *Embed quality* |
| Resource Management | **Resources** domain (*Develop Team* and *Manage Team* merge into *Lead the Team*) |
| Communications Management | **Stakeholders** domain |
| Risk Management | **Risk** domain (qualitative and quantitative analysis merge into *Perform Risk Analysis*) |
| Procurement Management | Sourcing strategy in **Governance** (*Plan Sourcing Strategy*); acquisition and control through the **Resources** domain; strategic detail in **Appendix X4** |
| Stakeholder Management | **Stakeholders** domain |

And the Seventh Edition's eight domains map like this: *Stakeholders → Stakeholders; Team → Resources; Development Approach & Life Cycle → Governance and tailoring; Planning → spread across all domains; Project Work → Governance and Resources; Delivery → Scope (plus value and quality); Measurement → Governance (monitoring) and each domain's control process; Uncertainty → Risk.* Schedule and Finance became domains in their own right.

## 2.4 Core vocabulary

You must be able to use these terms precisely — many distractors depend on confusing them.

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Project** | A **temporary** endeavour undertaken to create a **unique** product, service or result. Temporary = definite beginning and end (not short); unique = something not done exactly before. |
| **Progressive elaboration** | Developing plans and details iteratively as more information becomes available. |
| **Project management** | The application of knowledge, skills, tools and techniques to project activities to meet project requirements and deliver value. |
| **Operations** | Ongoing, repetitive work that sustains the organisation (e.g., running payroll). Projects often *change* operations. |
| **Program** | Related projects, subsidiary programs and program activities managed in a **coordinated** way to obtain **benefits** not available from managing them individually. |
| **Portfolio** | Projects, programs, subsidiary portfolios and operations managed **as a group** to achieve **strategic objectives**. Components need not be related to each other. |
| **Product** | An artifact that is produced, quantifiable, and can be an end item or a component. Products often outlive the projects that create or change them. |
| **Deliverable** | Any unique and verifiable product, result or capability required to complete a process, phase or project. |
| **Output** | What the project produces (a deliverable). |
| **Outcome** | The end result or consequence of using the output (e.g., faster order processing). |
| **Benefit** | A gain realised by the organisation and beneficiaries from the outcome (e.g., lower costs, higher customer satisfaction). |
| **Value** | The worth, importance or usefulness of something — financial or non-financial. Projects exist to create value. |
| **Organisational project management (OPM)** | A framework that integrates portfolio, program and project management with organisational enablers to achieve strategic objectives. |
| **Stakeholder** | An individual, group or organisation that may affect, be affected by, or perceive itself to be affected by a decision, activity or outcome of the project. |
| **Sponsor** | The person or group that provides resources and support for the project and is accountable for enabling success (and usually for benefits). |
| **Baseline** | The approved version of a work product (scope, schedule, cost) that can be changed only through formal change control and is used for comparison. |
| **Enterprise environmental factors (EEFs)** | Conditions *not under the immediate control of the team* that influence, constrain or direct the project (culture, regulations, market, infrastructure). |
| **Organisational process assets (OPAs)** | Plans, processes, policies, procedures and knowledge bases *specific to and used by* the performing organisation (templates, lessons learned, historical data). |
| **Tailoring** | Deliberate adaptation of approach, governance and processes to suit the context. |
| **Model / Method / Artifact** | A model is a thinking strategy to explain a process or framework; a method is a means of achieving an outcome (a technique); an artifact is a template, document, output or deliverable. |

### Project vs operations vs program vs portfolio

| Aspect | Project | Program | Portfolio | Operations |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Objective | Deliver specific outputs and outcomes | Deliver benefits through coordination | Achieve strategic objectives; optimise investment | Sustain the business |
| Duration | Temporary | Temporary (often long) | Ongoing | Ongoing |
| Change | Controlled through the plan | Expected; managed to optimise benefits | Monitored in the broader environment | Minimised; continuous improvement |
| Success measured by | Product and project quality, timeliness, budget, satisfaction, value | Benefits realised | Aggregate investment performance and benefits | Efficiency, KPIs |

### Why projects are started

Projects are vehicles for change. Common drivers:

- **Regulatory, legal or social requirements** (new safety law, accessibility rules);
- **Stakeholder requests or needs** (a customer asks for a new feature, a community requests a facility);
- **Business or technological strategies** (digital transformation, entering a new market, adopting AI);
- **Creating, improving or fixing products, processes or services** (fixing defects, improving performance).

> **Exam tip.** Questions often hide a portfolio or program concept inside a project scenario. If two projects compete for the same scarce resources and the decision affects strategy, the right level for the decision is usually **portfolio** governance, not the project manager.

Which combination correctly describes the structure of the PMBOK Guide – Eighth Edition?
A) 6 principles, 7 performance domains, 5 focus areas and 40 processes
B) 12 principles, 8 performance domains and models, methods and artifacts
C) 5 process groups, 10 knowledge areas and 49 processes

? The Eighth Edition is organised around 6 principles, 7 performance domains, 5 focus areas and 40 processes. Twelve principles and eight domains describe the Seventh Edition; ten knowledge areas and 49 processes describe the Sixth.

@@
Which of the following is NOT one of the seven performance domains of the Eighth Edition?
A) Quality
B) Finance
C) Governance
D) Resources
= A
? The seven domains are Governance, Scope, Schedule, Finance, Stakeholders, Resources and Risk. Quality is embedded across domains: planning in Define Scope, assurance in Governance and control in Monitor and Control Scope.

@@
A new IT system is delivered (output). Customer orders are now processed in four hours instead of two days (outcome). What is the best example of the resulting benefit?
A) Higher customer retention and lower processing costs
B) The installed and tested IT system, accepted by the customer
C) The project closure report
D) The trained help-desk team
= A
? The output is the system, the outcome is the faster processing, and the benefit is the gain the organisation realises from that outcome, such as retention and cost savings.

@@
What distinguishes a program from a portfolio?
A) A program coordinates related components for added benefits; a portfolio groups components to meet strategy
B) A program is ongoing, while a portfolio is temporary
C) A portfolio contains only projects, while a program contains only operations and maintenance work of the organisation
D) There is no difference; the terms are interchangeable
= A
? Programs are about coordination of related work for benefits; portfolios are about selecting and prioritising investments for strategy. Portfolio components need not be related.

@@
Which of the following is an organisational process asset rather than an enterprise environmental factor?
A) The lessons learned repository from previous projects
B) New government safety regulations
C) The organisation's culture and its appetite for risk in new projects
D) Current market exchange rates
= A
? OPAs are plans, processes, policies, procedures and knowledge bases owned by the performing organisation, such as historical information and lessons learned. Regulations, culture and market conditions are EEFs.

A) Stakeholders performance domain
B) Governance performance domain
C) Resources performance domain

? Plan Communications Management, Manage Communications and Monitor Communications are processes of the Stakeholders domain, reflecting the close link between communication and engagement.

A colleague says, "A project is temporary, so it must be short." What is the best response?
