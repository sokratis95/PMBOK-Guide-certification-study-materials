# Chapter 16 — Leading People: Vision, Teams, Conflict, Motivation, Influence and Ethics

**After this chapter you should be able to:**
- build a common vision and team agreements;
- choose a leadership style and source of power suited to the situation;
- apply motivation theories and team development models;
- select a conflict resolution approach based on the situation;
- use emotional intelligence, negotiation, decision-making and coaching techniques;
- apply the PMI Code of Ethics and Professional Conduct to dilemmas.

The People domain is 33% of the exam. Many of its questions have no "process" answer — they test judgment about human behaviour.

## 16.1 Developing a common vision (People task 1)

A shared vision aligns the team and stakeholders on **why** the project exists and **what success looks like**. Tools:

- **Project/product vision statement** — short, inspiring, memorable.
- **Elevator pitch** — *For* (target customer) *who* (need), *the* (product) *is a* (category) *that* (key benefit). *Unlike* (alternative), *our product* (differentiator).
- **Product box** — the team designs the "box" the product would be sold in, forcing focus on key benefits.
- **Project charter / team charter** — purpose, objectives, success criteria, values and working agreements.
- **Chartering workshops / inception** — collaborative sessions with stakeholders and team.
- **Roadmaps** — show how the vision will be reached over time.
- **Revisit the vision** regularly (sprint reviews, phase gates), especially when the team or context changes.

> **Exam tip.** If the team members disagree about priorities or "don't know why we are doing this", the best answer usually brings people back to the **vision, objectives and business value** — often by facilitating a session with the product owner or sponsor.

## 16.2 Leadership styles

| Style | Description | When useful |
|---|---|---|
| **Servant leadership** | Serves the team first: removes impediments, coaches, facilitates, empowers | Default for agile teams and knowledge work |
| **Transformational** | Inspires through vision, idealised attributes, individual consideration, intellectual stimulation | Change initiatives, motivating innovation |
| **Transactional** | Focuses on goals, feedback and rewards for performance (management by exception) | Routine work, clear targets |
| **Charismatic** | Inspires through energy and conviction | Rallying people; risk of dependence on the leader |
| **Interactional** | Combination of transactional, transformational and charismatic | Many situations |
| **Laissez-faire** | Hands-off; team makes decisions | Highly skilled, self-directed teams |
| **Directive / autocratic / command-and-control** | Leader decides and directs | Crises, safety emergencies, inexperienced teams in urgent situations |
| **Participative / democratic** | Involves the team in decisions | Building buy-in |
| **Situational leadership** (Hersey & Blanchard) | Adapt style to follower **competence** and **commitment** | Always — matching style to readiness |

**Situational Leadership® II** (Blanchard) maps four styles to four development levels:

| Development level | Typical state | Style |
|---|---|---|
| D1 — low competence, high commitment ("enthusiastic beginner") | New, eager | **S1 Directing** — high direction, low support |
| D2 — some competence, low commitment ("disillusioned learner") | Struggling | **S2 Coaching** — high direction, high support |
| D3 — moderate-high competence, variable commitment ("capable but cautious") | Able but hesitant | **S3 Supporting** — low direction, high support |
| D4 — high competence, high commitment ("self-reliant achiever") | Expert | **S4 Delegating** — low direction, low support |

**Leadership vs management:** management focuses on processes, systems, control and short-term results ("doing things right"); leadership focuses on people, vision, inspiration and change ("doing the right things"). Project managers need both.

## 16.3 Sources of power

| Power | Based on |
|---|---|
| **Formal / legitimate / positional** | The PM's assigned position (charter) |
| **Reward** | Ability to give rewards |
| **Penalty / coercive** | Ability to impose negative consequences — **least effective** in the long term |
| **Expert** | Knowledge, skills, experience — **among the most effective** |
| **Referent** | Respect, admiration, credibility — people want to follow you — **among the most effective** |
| **Informational** | Control over gathering or distribution of information |
| **Situational** | Gained because of unique circumstances (e.g., a crisis) |
| **Relational** | Networks, connections and alliances |
| **Personal / charismatic** | Charm, attraction |
| **Ingratiating** | Flattery or other common ground (manipulative — avoid) |
| **Guilt-based / persuasive / avoiding** | Other forms described in PMI materials |

> **Exam tip.** The PM's most effective forms of power are generally **expert** and **referent**; the least effective is **coercive/penalty**. In matrix organisations the PM has limited formal power, so influence, negotiation and relationship-building matter more.

## 16.4 Motivation theories

| Theory | Key idea | Practical meaning |
|---|---|---|
| **Maslow's hierarchy of needs** | Physiological → safety → social/belonging → esteem → self-actualisation. Lower needs must be met before higher ones motivate | A worried employee (job security) won't be motivated by an award ceremony |
| **Herzberg's two-factor theory** | **Hygiene factors** (salary, working conditions, policies, job security, supervision) prevent dissatisfaction but **do not motivate**; **motivators** (achievement, recognition, responsibility, growth, the work itself) create satisfaction | Fix hygiene problems, then use motivators |
| **McGregor's Theory X and Y** | X: people dislike work, must be controlled (micromanagement). Y: people are self-motivated and seek responsibility (empowerment) | Agile assumes Theory Y |
| **Ouchi's Theory Z** | Long-term employment, collective decision-making, concern for employee well-being, loyalty | Japanese management style |
| **McClelland's achievement (acquired needs) theory** | Needs for **achievement**, **affiliation** and **power** differ by person | Give achievers challenging goals, affiliators teamwork, power-seekers leadership opportunities |
| **Vroom's expectancy theory** | Motivation = **expectancy** (effort → performance) × **instrumentality** (performance → reward) × **valence** (value of reward) | Rewards must be achievable and valued |
| **Daniel Pink — Drive** | Intrinsic motivation from **autonomy, mastery and purpose**; extrinsic rewards work for simple tasks but can hurt creative work | Empower teams, support learning, connect work to purpose |
| **Equity theory** | People compare their input/outcome ratio with others' | Perceived unfairness demotivates |

**Recognition and rewards:** timely, specific, sincere, appropriate to the person and culture (some people dislike public praise). Avoid **win-lose** rewards that only one person can win when you want teamwork.

## 16.5 Team development

### Tuckman's ladder

1. **Forming** — team meets, learns about the project and roles; people are polite, independent, unsure. *Leader:* directive; clarify goals, roles, ground rules.
2. **Storming** — conflicts emerge over approaches and roles; if not handled constructively, the team can regress. *Leader:* coach, facilitate conflict resolution.
3. **Norming** — team adjusts behaviours, develops trust and working habits. *Leader:* support, facilitate.
4. **Performing** — high-functioning, interdependent, self-managing. *Leader:* delegate, remove impediments.
5. **Adjourning** — work completed, team released (also called *mourning*). *Leader:* recognise, celebrate, capture lessons, support transition.

Teams can move back to an earlier stage when membership or circumstances change.

### Drexler/Sibbet team performance model

Seven stages: **orientation** (why am I here?), **trust building** (who are you?), **goal clarification** (what are we doing?), **commitment** (how will we do it?), **implementation** (who does what, when, where?), **high performance** (wow!), **renewal** (why continue?).

### Building high-performing teams

- Clear vision and objectives, **shared ownership**, **trust**, **open communication**, **constructive disagreement**, **psychological safety**, **empowerment**, **recognition**, **collaboration**, accountability, **continuous learning** (training, cross-training, mentoring, pairing).
- **Team charter / working agreements**; **ground rules** (e.g., cameras on in decision meetings, response times).
- **Co-location** (war room) or effective **virtual team** practices: overlap hours, clear communication protocols, digital collaboration tools, occasional in-person meetings, inclusive meeting practices across time zones and cultures.
- **Diversity, equity and inclusion** — diverse teams make better decisions when everyone is heard; leaders ensure inclusive participation.

## 16.6 Conflict management

**Common sources of project conflict** (in descending frequency in classic research): **schedules**, **project priorities**, **resources**, technical opinions, administrative procedures, cost, personalities. Personality conflicts are usually the *least* frequent root cause.

**Conflict is normal** and can be healthy (constructive debate improves solutions). Handle it early, privately when possible, with focus on issues not personalities, and on the present and future, not the past.

### Five resolution techniques (Thomas–Kilmann / PMI)

| Technique | Description | Result | When appropriate |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Collaborate / problem-solve** | Incorporate multiple viewpoints; work openly to find a solution that satisfies all; requires trust and time | **Win-win**; consensus and commitment | Usually the **best** when time permits and the issue is important |
| **Compromise / reconcile** | Each party gives up something to reach a temporary or partial solution | **Lose-lose** (or partial win-win) | When collaboration fails, parties have equal power, or time is short |
| **Smooth / accommodate** | Emphasise areas of agreement, downplay differences; concede one's position | Lose-win; temporary peace | To preserve relationships, when the issue matters more to the other party |
| **Force / direct** | Push one viewpoint at the expense of others (using power) | Win-lose | Emergencies, safety, legal or ethical issues, when unpopular decisions are needed quickly |
| **Withdraw / avoid** | Retreat or postpone | Lose-lose (temporary) | When emotions are high and a cooling-off period helps, or the issue is trivial — never a long-term solution |

**Levels of conflict (Speed Leas):** 1 — problem to solve (information sharing, collaboration); 2 — disagreement (personal protection appears; support and empowerment help); 3 — contest (winning matters; use accommodation, negotiation); 4 — crusade/fight (protecting one's own group; diplomacy needed); 5 — world war (destroy the other; do whatever is necessary to prevent harm). The approach depends on the level.

> **Exam tip.** When two team members argue, the PM usually **first lets them try to resolve it themselves**, then **facilitates a private conversation** focused on the issue (collaborate). Escalating to functional managers or HR is for persistent or serious issues (harassment, ethics violations). In agile teams, the team is encouraged to resolve conflicts itself, with the scrum master/servant leader facilitating.

## 16.7 Emotional intelligence (EI)

The ability to identify, assess and manage one's own emotions and those of others. Goleman's model:

| Component | Focus |
|---|---|
| **Self-awareness** | Recognising one's own emotions, strengths, weaknesses |
| **Self-regulation (self-management)** | Controlling impulses, staying calm, adapting |
| **Motivation** | Inner drive toward goals |
| **Empathy** | Understanding others' feelings and perspectives |
| **Social skills** | Managing relationships, building networks, influencing |

Self-awareness and self-regulation are about **yourself**; empathy and social skills about **others**. High EI leaders handle stress, conflict and change better.

## 16.8 Decision-making

| Method | Description |
|---|---|
| **Unanimity** | Everyone agrees (Delphi can help) |
| **Majority** | More than 50% support |
| **Plurality** | The largest block decides even without a majority |
| **Autocratic** | One person decides (appropriate in emergencies or when it is clearly that person's decision) |
| **Consensus** | Everyone can live with and support the decision |
| **Fist of five / fist-to-five** | Each person shows 0–5 fingers; low counts trigger discussion |
| **Roman voting** | Thumbs up, sideways, down |
| **Multicriteria decision analysis** | Weighted criteria matrix to evaluate options |
| **Delphi** | Anonymous rounds with experts; reduces bias and groupthink |
| **Nominal group technique** | Ideas generated individually, then ranked |

**Divergent then convergent thinking:** generate options broadly (brainstorm), then narrow down. **Decide at the last responsible moment** for uncertain decisions (agile).

## 16.9 Negotiation and influencing

- Aim for **win-win** outcomes (principled negotiation — Fisher & Ury: separate people from the problem; focus on interests, not positions; invent options for mutual gain; insist on objective criteria).
- Know your **BATNA** (best alternative to a negotiated agreement) — your walk-away point; and the **ZOPA** (zone of possible agreement).
- **Influencing** without authority: build relationships and trust, understand others' interests, use data and logic, reciprocity, coalitions, credibility (expert/referent power).
- **Political awareness** — understand power structures and informal networks.

## 16.10 Coaching, mentoring and knowledge transfer (People task 7)

- **Coaching** — helps a person develop their own solutions through questions and feedback (performance-oriented, often short-term). **OSCAR model**: Outcome, Situation, Choices/consequences, Actions, Review.
- **Mentoring** — a more experienced person shares knowledge and guidance (career-oriented, longer-term).
- **Training** — formal learning (classroom, e-learning, on-the-job); planned in the resource management plan; costs included in the budget.
- **Knowledge transfer techniques** — pairing, job shadowing, communities of practice, lessons learned sessions, documentation and wikis, demos, "lunch and learn", handover workshops with operations, rotating roles, storytelling.
- Knowledge transfer happens **throughout** the project (not only at the end) and into **operations** and **future projects**.

## 16.11 Cultural awareness

- Cultures differ on power distance, individualism/collectivism, uncertainty avoidance, time orientation, communication style (direct/indirect, high-context/low-context) — e.g., Hofstede's dimensions.
- Practices: learn about team members' cultures, avoid assumptions and stereotypes, use clear language, confirm understanding, respect holidays and time zones, adapt recognition and feedback styles.

## 16.12 The PMI Code of Ethics and Professional Conduct

The Code describes expectations for PMI members and certification holders in four values. Each has **aspirational standards** (what we strive for) and **mandatory standards** (firm requirements; violations can lead to disciplinary action).

| Value | Aspirational examples | Mandatory examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Responsibility** | Make decisions in the best interests of society, safety and environment; accept only assignments consistent with our skills; fulfil commitments; own errors | **Follow policies, rules, laws**; **report unethical or illegal conduct** (backed by facts); bring violations to the attention of appropriate authorities |
| **Respect** | Learn about others' norms and customs; listen; approach conflicts directly; conduct ourselves professionally | **Negotiate in good faith**; do not exercise power to influence others for personal benefit; do not act abusively; respect others' property rights |
| **Fairness** | Be transparent in decision-making; examine impartiality; provide equal access to information; equal opportunities | **Disclose conflicts of interest** and refrain from decisions when conflicted; do not hire/fire, reward/punish based on personal considerations (favouritism, nepotism, bribery); do not discriminate |
| **Honesty** | Seek to understand the truth; be truthful in communications; provide accurate, timely information; make commitments in good faith | **Do not engage in deceptive behaviour** (false statements, half-truths, information out of context); do not act dishonestly for personal gain |

**Typical ethical scenarios and responses:**
- Gifts from vendors: follow company policy and the law; decline gifts that could influence decisions; disclose.
- Discovering a team member's falsified test results: address it, report through appropriate channels, correct the record.
- Being asked to misreport status: refuse; report accurately; discuss with the sponsor.
- Conflict of interest (e.g., a relative's company bids): **disclose** it and remove yourself from the decision.
- Local customs (e.g., facilitation payments) vs law: **follow the law** (anti-bribery laws apply even abroad) and company policy.
- Copyright/software licences: respect intellectual property.

A) Meet with both to understand their positions and help them find a solution

C) Report both team members to their functional managers
