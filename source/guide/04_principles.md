# Chapter 4 — The Six Principles of Project Management

**After this chapter you should be able to:**
- state the six principles of the Eighth Edition and explain each in your own words;
- recognise behaviours that apply (or violate) each principle in a scenario;
- relate the six principles to the twelve principles of the Seventh Edition and to the PMI Code of Ethics.

## 4.1 What a principle is (and is not)

A **principle** is a fundamental norm, truth or value that guides behaviour and decisions. Principles are **not prescriptive**: they do not tell you which template to use. They apply to every project, every development approach, every industry. When a scenario seems to have no "process" answer, ask yourself which principle the best action would honour.

The principles are consistent with the **PMI Code of Ethics and Professional Conduct** (responsibility, respect, fairness and honesty — see Chapter 16). They also overlap: one good action usually honours several principles at once.

## 4.2 Principle 1 — Adopt a holistic view

**Idea in one sentence:** see the project as a system of interacting parts that lives inside larger systems (the organisation, the market, society), and make decisions with the whole in mind.

**Key ideas**
- **Systems thinking.** A change in one area ripples elsewhere: compressing the schedule may raise cost, risk and team stress; a supplier change may affect quality and compliance.
- **Integration across domains.** Scope, schedule, finance, resources, risk and stakeholders are interdependent — the seven domains are *interactive, interrelated and interdependent*.
- **Life-cycle perspective.** Consider the product's whole life — operation, maintenance, support, disposal — not only the project's delivery.
- **Complexity awareness.** Many sources of complexity (human behaviour, system behaviour, ambiguity, technological innovation) cannot be removed by planning harder; they require sensing, adapting and feedback.
- **Context.** The organisation's strategy, culture, governance and environment shape what "good" looks like.

**In practice:** impact analysis before approving changes; cross-functional reviews; considering downstream users and operations; tracking interdependencies with other projects and programs.

**Violations to recognise:** optimising one constraint at the expense of the whole ("we met the date, the help desk can deal with the defects"); ignoring effects on other projects that share resources.

## 4.3 Principle 2 — Focus on value

**Idea in one sentence:** continually evaluate and adjust the project so that it delivers the intended outcomes, benefits and value — not just outputs.

**Key ideas**
- **Value is the ultimate success indicator.** It may be financial or non-financial, and may be realised during or after the project.
- **Clarify value early** with stakeholders: what problem are we solving, for whom, and how will we know?
- **Prioritise by value.** In adaptive work the product owner orders the backlog by value; in predictive work the business case and benefits plan anchor decisions.
- **Deliver early and often** where possible, so value is realised sooner and assumptions are validated by real feedback.
- **Re-evaluate continuously.** If the business case is no longer valid, the right decision may be to change direction — or to stop the project.

**In practice:** minimum viable products (MVPs); value-based prioritisation (MoSCoW, cost of delay, weighted shortest job first); benefit KPIs; phase-gate reviews of the business case.

**Violations:** continuing a project that no longer makes sense because "we already spent so much" (sunk-cost thinking); measuring success purely by "on time, on budget".

## 4.4 Principle 3 — Embed quality into processes and deliverables

**Idea in one sentence:** quality is designed and built in from the beginning — in the way work is done and in what is produced — rather than inspected in at the end.

**Key ideas**
- **Two dimensions of quality:** the *process* (how we work) and the *product* (what we deliver). Both must meet the expectations of stakeholders and the acceptance criteria.
- **Prevention over inspection.** Preventing a defect is cheaper than finding it; finding it early is cheaper than finding it late.
- **Quality is defined by requirements and fitness for use**, including non-functional requirements (performance, security, usability, sustainability).
- **Everyone owns quality.** Teams use definitions of done, peer reviews, test automation and standards; leaders create conditions for doing it right.
- **Continuous improvement.** Retrospectives, lessons learned, root-cause analysis and PDCA cycles improve both process and product.

**Why it matters for the Eighth Edition structure:** quality is no longer a separate domain. **Quality planning** is part of *Define Scope*, **quality assurance** is the Governance process *Manage Quality Assurance*, and **quality control** is part of *Monitor and Control Scope*. The principle explains why: quality is everyone's responsibility in every domain.

**Violations:** skipping testing to meet a date; adding extra features nobody asked for (gold plating — more is not "higher quality"); treating quality as the QA department's problem.

## 4.5 Principle 4 — Be an accountable leader

**Idea in one sentence:** take ownership of decisions, actions and results; lead with integrity and transparency, whatever your formal role.

**Key ideas**
- **Leadership is not a title.** Anyone on the team can demonstrate leadership behaviours: setting direction, motivating, facilitating, taking ownership.
- **Accountability and ownership.** Leaders own outcomes, admit mistakes, report status honestly and follow through on commitments.
- **Stewardship.** Act as a responsible steward of the organisation's resources and of the trust of stakeholders: integrity, care, trustworthiness and compliance with laws and policies.
- **Ethics.** Follow the PMI Code of Ethics; act transparently; avoid conflicts of interest; protect confidential information.
- **Adapt the style.** Use servant leadership to empower teams, directive leadership in a crisis, coaching for less experienced members (situational leadership).
- **Decision-making.** Make timely, informed decisions at the right level, and know when a decision belongs to someone else (product owner, sponsor, CCB).

**In practice:** honest status reports (including bad news, early); escalating through the agreed path when a decision exceeds your authority; protecting the team from inappropriate pressure; modelling the behaviours you expect.

**Violations:** hiding problems ("watermelon" status — green outside, red inside); blaming team members publicly; accepting gifts that create conflicts of interest; making decisions that belong to the customer or product owner.

## 4.6 Principle 5 — Integrate sustainability within all project areas

**Idea in one sentence:** consider the long-term economic, environmental and social impacts of the project and its product in every decision.

**Key ideas**
- **The triple bottom line:** **people** (social), **planet** (environmental) and **prosperity/profit** (economic). Many organisations frame this as **ESG** — environmental, social and governance.
- **Across all domains:** sustainable sourcing (Resources/Governance), life-cycle costs and green finance (Finance), environmental and social risks (Risk), community and stakeholder impacts (Stakeholders), sustainable requirements and acceptance criteria (Scope), energy-efficient scheduling and logistics (Schedule).
- **Product life cycle:** design for reuse, recycling and minimal waste (circular economy); consider operation and disposal.
- **Social sustainability includes the team:** sustainable pace, well-being, fair labour practices, diversity and inclusion.
- **Compliance and beyond.** Meet legal requirements (environmental permits, reporting rules) and the organisation's sustainability commitments (e.g., net-zero targets, UN Sustainable Development Goals).

**In practice:** including sustainability criteria in supplier selection; measuring the carbon footprint of a solution; engaging affected communities; choosing options with lower life-cycle impact even when initial cost is slightly higher, when the business case supports it.

**Violations:** ignoring environmental regulations to save time; selecting a supplier with known unethical labour practices because it is cheapest; consistently demanding overtime ("death marches").

## 4.7 Principle 6 — Build an empowered culture

**Idea in one sentence:** create an environment of trust, psychological safety and shared ownership in which people and stakeholders can contribute their best, make decisions and adapt.

**Key ideas**
- **Empowerment.** Push decisions to the people closest to the work; give teams autonomy within clear boundaries; self-organising teams decide *how* to do their work.
- **Psychological safety.** People can raise problems, admit mistakes and challenge ideas without fear.
- **Collaboration and shared ownership.** Team agreements (team charter, ground rules), collective accountability for results.
- **Engaged stakeholders.** Stakeholders participate actively rather than receiving reports passively.
- **Diversity, equity and inclusion.** Diverse perspectives improve decisions; inclusive practices ensure all voices are heard.
- **Learning and adaptability.** A culture that experiments, learns from failure, and adapts to change is more resilient.

**In practice:** servant leadership; facilitating rather than dictating; recognising contributions; retrospectives with real follow-through; coaching; delegating decisions with clear guardrails.

**Violations:** micromanagement; punishing people who report bad news; assigning tasks to self-organising teams; excluding remote team members from decisions.

## 4.8 How the twelve principles of the Seventh Edition live on

This mapping is an interpretive study aid (the Eighth Edition does not require you to memorise it), but it helps if your other materials still use the Seventh-Edition list:

| Seventh Edition principle | Mainly reflected in (Eighth Edition) |
|---|---|
| Be a diligent, respectful and caring steward | Be an accountable leader; Integrate sustainability |
| Create a collaborative project team environment | Build an empowered culture |
| Effectively engage with stakeholders | Build an empowered culture; Stakeholders domain |
| Focus on value | Focus on value |
| Recognise, evaluate and respond to system interactions | Adopt a holistic view |
| Demonstrate leadership behaviours | Be an accountable leader |
| Tailor based on context | Tailoring (Guide); Adopt a holistic view |
| Build quality into processes and deliverables | Embed quality into processes and deliverables |
| Navigate complexity | Adopt a holistic view |
| Optimise risk responses | Risk performance domain |
| Embrace adaptability and resiliency | Build an empowered culture; Adopt a holistic view |
| Enable change to achieve the envisioned future state | Focus on value; Build an empowered culture (and organisational change, Chapter 17) |

## 4.9 Using principles to answer exam questions

When two answers both look reasonable, check which one better reflects the principles:

| If the scenario is about… | Prefer the answer that… |
|---|---|
| A change with ripple effects | analyses the impact across scope, schedule, cost, risk, quality and other projects (holistic view) |
| Continuing or stopping work | revisits value, the business case and benefits (focus on value) |
| Defects found late | addresses process causes and prevention, not just rework (embed quality) |
| A mistake or bad news | is transparent, owns the problem and acts ethically (accountable leader) |
| Environmental or social impacts | considers long-term impacts and stakeholders affected (sustainability) |
| Team decisions or morale | empowers, facilitates and builds safety (empowered culture) |

@@
A project manager proposes to compress the schedule by adding a night shift. Before approving, the PM asks the team to analyse effects on quality, safety, costs, team well-being and the neighbouring community. Which principle is most directly demonstrated?
A) Adopt a holistic view
B) Focus on value
C) Build an empowered culture
D) Embed quality into processes and deliverables
= A
? Considering the ripple effects of one decision on several areas and on the wider environment is systems thinking, the core of adopting a holistic view.

@@
Market research shows that the product being built will no longer meet customers' needs. The project is 60% complete and on budget. What does the principle "Focus on value" suggest?
A) Re-evaluate the business case with the sponsor and consider changing direction
B) Finish the project, because 60% of the budget has already been spent and must not be wasted
C) Reduce testing to finish faster
D) Ask the team to add more features to increase value
= A
? Value, not completion, is the measure of success. Continuing only because of money already spent is sunk-cost thinking; the right action is to revisit the business case with the decision-maker.

@@
Which practice best reflects the principle "Embed quality into processes and deliverables"?
A) Agreeing on a definition of done and automated tests before development starts
B) Inspecting all of the deliverables thoroughly at the end of the project, before handover
C) Adding features beyond the requirements to delight the customer
D) Letting the quality department own all quality decisions
= A
? Embedding quality means building it in from the start through clear criteria and preventive practices. Final inspection alone is detection, gold plating adds risk without value, and quality is everyone's responsibility.

@@
A project manager discovers that a status report sent last week understated a cost overrun. What should the project manager do, consistent with being an accountable leader?
A) Inform the sponsor promptly, correct the report and explain the planned actions
B) Wait until the overrun has been recovered, so that the issue never needs to be reported to anyone
C) Ask the finance analyst to take responsibility for the error
D) Remove cost information from future reports
= A
? Accountable leaders are transparent and own their mistakes. Correcting the record promptly preserves trust; hiding or shifting blame violates the Code of Ethics.

@@
During supplier selection, the lowest bidder uses a manufacturing process with significant environmental damage, while the second-lowest bidder meets the organisation's environmental commitments at a 3% higher price. Which principle should most influence the recommendation?
A) Integrate sustainability within all project areas
B) Focus on value, so price is the only criterion
C) Adopt a holistic view, so the cheapest option is preferred
D) Build an empowered culture
= A
? Sustainability requires considering environmental and social impacts in sourcing decisions. Value is broader than price, and a holistic view would also consider reputational and regulatory risk.

@@
An agile team asks the project manager to decide how to split a feature into technical tasks. What best reflects the principle "Build an empowered culture"?
A) Encourage the team to decide the breakdown and offer support
B) Prepare the task list and assign tasks to each developer
C) Ask the sponsor to decide the breakdown
D) Postpone the feature until the project manager has time to analyse it in detail
= A
? Self-organising teams decide how to do their work. The PM supports and coaches rather than dictating, which builds ownership and capability.

@@
A product owner wants to measure the success of a new self-service portal. Which metric best reflects the principle of focusing on value?
A) Lower call-centre volume and higher customer satisfaction after launch
B) Number of lines of code written

D) Number of status meetings held
= A
? Value-oriented metrics measure outcomes and benefits for the organisation and customers. Code volume, spend and meeting counts measure activity, not value.

Which statement about the six principles is correct?
